Nvidia reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO after Trump says he “convinced” the board, and Congress lines up questions

Official statementAllegationBBC News, CBC News and 1 more · Nov 23–24 (Huang resigned Nov 12)

Nvidia’s board gave Jensen Huang his job back on Monday, Nov. 23, less than two weeks after he resigned over an affair with a woman some U.S. intelligence officials allege was a Chinese spy. President Trump says he persuaded the board.

Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisThis is about more than one CEO. A president personally steering who runs America’s most valuable chip company, while vouching for that person on national security, mixes politics, markets and intelligence in an unusual way. Nvidia’s chips power the AI boom, so its leadership moves retirement savings and the wider stock market. Next week’s congressional hearings are the first public test of what the government actually knows.
Full story, sources & checks6 sources

Nvidia’s board gave Jensen Huang his job back on Monday, Nov. 23, less than two weeks after he resigned over an affair with a woman some U.S. intelligence officials allege was a Chinese spy. President Trump says he persuaded the board. Huang, the chipmaker’s co-founder, stepped down on Nov. 12 after admitting the relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), a businesswoman tied to the Singapore-based data-centre firm Megaspeed; China calls the spying accusations groundless. Only last week the board had said it did not plan to bring him back. On Monday chairman Harvey Jones said the reinstatement was in the “best interests” of shareholders, staff and partners, and the board said input from senior U.S. officials left it confident Huang posed no national-security risk. Trump told CNBC: “I spoke to them… And I convinced them.” He said Attorney General Todd Blanche had confirmed the government has no evidence Huang broke the law. Nvidia shares jumped about 31%, adding close to $1 trillion in market value and lifting the Nasdaq 6.5%, though the stock was still about 21% below its level a month ago, the BBC reported. Also on Monday, the Commerce Department said it plans to put Megaspeed on its trade blacklist. Huang is scheduled to face the Senate banking committee on Dec. 3 and to give transcribed interviews to several House committees. Democratic senator Chris Van Hollen called the reinstatement “exhibit A” of “political corruption”, and lawmakers from both parties are expected to press Huang on his China ties.

Note: Updates the Nov 23 edition, which reported a (Reuters, unconfirmed by the BBC) SEC inquiry into Nvidia’s accounting. That inquiry is still reported only by outlets citing unnamed sources. The spying accusation against Alice Huang comes from intelligence reports described to journalists, not from charges; China denies it. Hearing dates are scheduled, not yet held.

A U.S. Department of War document posted on Monday, Nov. 23, says Canada is not meeting its NORAD or NATO obligations and has no funded plan to reach NATO’s 5% defence-spending target by 2035. The fact sheet, first reported by Axios on Sunday, says only 41.9% of the Royal Canadian Air Force’s fleet is ready for operations this budget year, cites low army and navy readiness and notes that just one of Canada’s four Victoria-class submarines is seaworthy at a time. It accuses Ottawa of prioritising “parochial economic and commercial interests over military optimization”, which it says will “further burden… the American taxpayer”, and points to Prime Minister Mark Carney’s ongoing review of the F-35 purchase. It also says Canada is deferring the biggest spending increases until after the 2029 federal election. The document follows a Washington Post report that War Secretary Pete Hegseth issued a classified memo restricting U.S. military contact with Canada; U.S. Senator Mike Rounds called those limits largely symbolic. Defence Minister David McGuinty’s office was not immediately available for comment, CBC reported, and did not respond to the Globe and Mail late Monday.

But declining to hold an official memorial, while warning about people who “make trouble”, signals how carefully authorities are managing public grief, after a book launch with migrant survivors was abruptly called off (Nov 23 edition).

Ahead of the first anniversary of the Wang Fuk Court fire on Nov. 26, the Hong Kong government said on Tuesday, Nov. 24 (Hong Kong time), that it will not host official memorial events, and announced two new grants for victims worth about HK$630 million. Chief Secretary Eric Chan told Commercial Radio that many community and religious memorials are already planned, that the government would not obstruct lawful ones and might help with transport, but urged people to choose “carefully” because some online were trying to use memorials to “make trouble”, Hong Kong Free Press reported. Under the new “New Beginning” grants, each flat owner gets HK$300,000 in two instalments (end of December 2026 and end of June 2027), and the family of each person who died gets HK$200,000 in December, paid through social workers without applications. Families of foreign domestic helpers who died will be contacted through consulates. Secretary for Home and Youth Affairs Alice Mak said the HK$4.5 billion support fund is now largely used up. The fire spread across seven blocks of the estate in Tai Po during renovation work in November 2025, killing 168 people.

The defence ministry said it would summon Russia’s defence attaché to protest. Ulleung, a volcanic island of about 9,000 people, lies roughly 120 km off the east coast.

A federal appeals court on Monday, Nov. 23, let the Army go ahead with blowing up 68 drums of degraded lead azide at a plant in Middletown, Iowa, hours after President Trump had ordered a pause. New York caps Citi Bike member e-bike rides at $3.65 and freezes membership prices through 2029

Official statementNYC Mayor’s Office (press release), Gothamist and 1 more · Nov 23 (cap starts Jan 19, 2027)

From Jan. 19, 2027, Citi Bike members will pay no more than $3.65 for an e-bike ride of up to 45 minutes, under a deal Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced with Lyft on Monday, Nov. 23.

File photo, 2020 · Photo: Jim.henderson · CC BY 4.0

More3 sources
Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisFor anyone who bikes to class or work, a long e-bike ride drops from about $12 to under $4, which makes Citi Bike a real alternative to the subway for some trips. The city pays nothing directly; it gives up ad space and revenue instead. The catch is the price freeze ends in 2029, when the contract is renegotiated.

From Jan. 19, 2027, Citi Bike members will pay no more than $3.65 for an e-bike ride of up to 45 minutes, under a deal Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced with Lyft on Monday, Nov. 23. Members now pay 27 cents a minute on e-bikes, or $12.15 for a 45-minute ride, except trips into or out of Manhattan, which are capped at $5.40; the new cap replaces that one. The annual membership fee ($239) and member per-minute and overage fees are frozen immediately until May 26, 2029, when Lyft’s contract with the city ends, the mayor’s office said. Riders in the Reduced Fare Bike Share program are covered too. In return the city will make it easier to install charging at more than 130 stations from early 2027, let Lyft sell ads on station panels from Jan. 1, and change revenue sharing so Lyft pays the city only above a $5.25 million yearly threshold. Prices for non-members are not covered by the agreement, Gothamist reported. Mamdani said the cap would have saved riders about $11 million last year.

Note: The Guardian’s report gives the revenue-share threshold as “$5.25bn”; the mayor’s office transcript and other reports say $5.25 million, which is used here.
