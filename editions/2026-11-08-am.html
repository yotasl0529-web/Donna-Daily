Gunman kills six in Kraków’s Main Market Square in what Poland’s prime minister calls terrorism

Official statementBBC News, DW · Nov 7

A gunman driving through Kraków’s historic Main Market Square on Saturday afternoon fired at random people, killing six and injuring 16, police said. Prime Minister Donald Tusk called it a terrorist attack.

Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisMass shootings are rare in Poland, and an attack on one of Europe’s busiest tourist squares by a suspect officials link to far-right ideas will put extremism and gun access at the centre of Polish and EU security debates.
Full story, sources & checks2 sources

A gunman driving through Kraków’s historic Main Market Square on Saturday afternoon fired at random people, killing six and injuring 16, police said. Prime Minister Donald Tusk called it a terrorist attack. Police say the attack began shortly before 3 p.m. local time and lasted about three minutes; officers chased the car until it crashed, then shot and arrested the suspect, a 25-year-old Polish man who is in hospital after surgery. Four women and two men were killed — three Polish, one British and two not yet identified, according to the police chief — and five of the injured are foreign nationals. Two police officers were wounded, one critically. Tusk said three more bodies, two women and a man, were found at the suspect’s home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, with “strong indications” they were his immediate family. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński said the suspect appeared “fascinated by far-right ideologies” and held “ultra-nationalist” views. Kraków declared mourning until Nov. 11.

Note: Details such as the suspect’s motive come from Polish officials and are not yet established in court. The suspect has not been officially named. BBC reports mourning in Kraków until Nov. 11; DW reports Tusk announced two days of national mourning.

CBC News · Nov 7

Emergency physicians across Canada say medetomidine, a veterinary sedative not approved for humans, is turning up in street fentanyl and making overdoses and withdrawal harder to treat, CBC News reported.

More1 source
Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisA new adulterant that naloxone can’t reverse changes the basic first-aid advice for overdoses and adds strain to already crowded emergency rooms.

Emergency physicians across Canada say medetomidine, a veterinary sedative not approved for humans, is turning up in street fentanyl and making overdoses and withdrawal harder to treat, CBC News reported. First detected in Canada in 2023, it has increased substantially in B.C. and Ontario and risen sharply this year in Saskatchewan and Yukon, according to the Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction. Naloxone reverses opioids but not medetomidine, so responders are told to focus on breathing. Doctors describe severe withdrawal — racing heart, high blood pressure, vomiting, delirium — that can require hospital or intensive care. Some physicians called for more supervised consumption sites; Ontario’s health ministry pointed to its new treatment hubs instead.

How to read the labels

Reported Reported by the linked news outlets; attributed to them, not independently confirmed by Donna Daily.

Official statement A government, official body or company's own statement, figures or proposal, attributed to it.

Claim An assertion by a person, government or group that hasn't been independently confirmed.

Allegation Claimed, charged or argued in court; not proven.

Analysis Interpretation or opinion — the outlet's, an expert's, or Donna Daily's own — marked as such.

Unnamed sources Rests partly on sources the outlet did not name.

State media Relies on state-controlled media (e.g. Xinhua, CCTV, Russian state media).

Labels describe the type of claim a story rests on; they are not a verification grade.
