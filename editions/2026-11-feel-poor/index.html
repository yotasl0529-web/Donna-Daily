Cheap TVs, dear homes: why rich countries feel poor

Factory goods got far cheaper; homes near good jobs and care did not. Independent data back much of that story, but not all of it.

AnalysisAnalysis · Nov 5, 2026
Why it matters
If the squeeze is about scarce homes and care rather than money printing or greed, the fixes are slower and more political: planning, building and how care is paid for.
Read more· full story & 6 sources

A widely shared explainer from the YouTube channel OBF argues that rich economies are very good at making factory goods plentiful and bad at making land near good jobs, or people’s time, plentiful. Donna Daily checked the core claims against other sources. On housing, they largely hold. Harvard’s Joint Center for Housing Studies puts the typical US home at 4.7 times median household income in 2025, against an average of 3.2 in the 1990s. Britain’s statistics office says a typical home in England costs 7.6 times full-time earnings, roughly double the 1997 ratio. On care, Child Care Aware of America puts average US child care at $13,184 a year, about 10% of a two-parent household’s median income. The mood fits too. Michigan’s consumer sentiment index was 48.0 in October, 10% below a year earlier, and a Harris poll for the Guardian found 61% of Americans wrongly believe the US is already in recession. Two cautions apply. English and US price-to-income ratios have eased since 2021–22. And part of this year’s gloom comes from a fresh shock, the Iran war’s energy spike, rather than from decades of scarcity.

Note: The video uses its own price-to-income measure (household income); Harvard JCHS and the ONS use different definitions, so their numbers are not directly comparable with each other or with the video’s.

The explainer’s case: no single villain, just scarce land and scarce time

OBF’s video tests six popular explanations across four countries and finds that none fits all of them alone. Its answer is that rich economies leave land near jobs, and people’s time, scarce.

AnalysisThe video’s argument
Why it matters
It is a clear, testable framework. The rest of this edition checks its main numbers against other sources and sets out where economists disagree.
Read more· full story & 1 source

The video starts from a paradox: many people in rich countries expect their children to be worse off, even as official growth forecasts rise. It then compares the US, Britain, Canada and Germany. Since 2000, it says, goods such as televisions have become far cheaper in hours of work, but housing has not. By its count, a home cost about three years of household income around 1990, against about 4.5 in the US, 5.2 in Britain and 5.4 in Canada today. Care is the second big cost: nursery and university in Britain, health insurance in the US, an incomplete $10-a-day childcare program in Canada, and in Germany near-free university paid for through high social contributions. It then tests six suspects: money printing and post-2022 inflation, a “vibecession,” weak wages, greedy companies, boomers and taxes. Each explains part of the picture, it argues, but none explains all four countries. It leaves immigration out so that all four countries are compared on the same terms. Its conclusion is that productivity growth makes manufactured goods abundant but cannot easily make land or caring time abundant, a version of economist William Baumol’s “cost disease.” Each country then adds its own twist: inequality in the US, stagnant pay in Britain, decline relative to the US and household debt in Canada, and care costs hidden in payroll charges in Germany.

Note: This is a summary of the video’s own argument. Figures here are the video’s unless another story in this edition re-checks them.

Housing · US

US homes cost nearly five years of income, against about three in the 1990s

Harvard’s housing center says the price-to-income ratio has hovered near five for five years, and the monthly payment on a typical home has nearly doubled since 2020.

ReportedData: Harvard JCHS, 2026 report
Why it matters
High prices and higher rates together are what lock people out. A fall in rates alone, the report notes, has given buyers only modest relief.
Read more· full story & 2 sources

The Joint Center for Housing Studies’ 2026 report says the median existing single-family home sold for 4.7 times median household income in 2025. That is down slightly from a record 5.0 in 2022 but up from 4.1 in 2019 and an average of 3.2 in the 1990s. Prices are up 54% nationally since 2020, or almost 25% after inflation. The monthly mortgage payment on a median-priced home, assuming 3.5% down and a 30-year fixed rate, was about $2,420 at the end of 2025, nearly double the $1,240 at the end of 2020, when rates were near record lows. There are signs of cooling: annual price growth slowed to 0.7% in February 2026, and 41 of the 100 largest metros saw outright declines in early 2026. The video’s figure of about 4.5 years of income is close to the center’s, though the two use different data.

Housing · UK

An English home costs 7.6 years of pay, about double 1997, though it has eased since 2021

Official figures show affordability improving as pay outpaces prices, but London remains far out of reach and private renters on lower incomes spend a large share of income on rent.

Official figuresData: ONS, March 2026
Why it matters
Britain shows that improving affordability is possible, through pay rises outpacing prices, but also how far it has to go.
Read more· full story & 4 sources

Britain’s Office for National Statistics says the median home in England, at £300,000, cost 7.6 times median full-time earnings in 2025, the lowest ratio since 2015, down from 7.8 in 2024. Since the 2021 peak, earnings have risen 25% while median prices have risen 5%. Over the long run the picture is starker: in 1997, a typical home in England and Wales cost three to four times a full-time salary. London stands at 10.6, and Kensington and Chelsea at 25.2. The ONS treats five times earnings as a broad affordability threshold. The Resolution Foundation, a think tank, says about 8.6 million lower-income Britons now rent privately, spending on average 43% of disposable income on housing. The ONS measures against one full-time worker’s earnings, while the video uses household income, which explains the gap between 7.6 and the video’s 5.2.

Why care keeps getting pricier: the “cost disease” behind nurseries and hospitals

When factories get more productive, wages rise everywhere. A nursery worker can’t care for many more toddlers, so care costs rise relative to goods. Economists argue over how much this explains.

AnalysisExplainer
Why it matters
If Baumol is right, care will keep getting relatively more expensive as countries get richer. The question becomes who pays, not how to make it cheap.
Read more· full story & 2 sources

The idea comes from the economist William Baumol. A string quartet takes as many players today as it did in 1800, but musicians’ pay has to keep pace with pay in industries where productivity soared. The same goes for childcare, nursing and teaching. George Mason economist Alex Tabarrok, co-author of a book on the effect, compared US price data and found that over 26 years the price of day care and preschool rose 36% relative to overall prices, while pet care, which is far less regulated, rose 28%. He argues that the similarity means regulation such as staff ratios is not the main driver of the slow, steady rise, though he accepts that regulation raises the level of prices. Other researchers put more weight on rules, workforce requirements and subsidies, and empirical tests of Baumol in health care have produced mixed results. The OBF video uses Baumol as half of its answer.

Childcare: high US prices, English subsidies and Canadian waitlists

Three countries are trying three different ways to pay for childcare. Each shows a different trade-off.

ReportedReports, 2025–2026
Why it matters
Lower fees help families who get a place. Without more staff and spaces, the shortage moves from price to availability, which Baumol’s logic would predict.
Read more· full story & 4 sources

United States: Child Care Aware of America says the average annual price was $13,184 in 2025, 10% of median income for a two-parent household and 33% for a single parent. In every state with data, center care for two children costs more than median rent. Britain: Coram’s 2026 survey says the price of a part-time nursery place for an under-two in England nearly tripled from £55 a week in 2001 to £160 in 2024. Expanded funded hours have since cut the cost of a full-time place by 39% for eligible working parents, but prices rose 5% in Scotland and 8% in Wales. Canada: the C.D. Howe Institute says eight provinces and territories have reached average fees of $10 a day or less and the rest have at least halved them, but waitlists are growing. More than three-quarters of centers reported waitlists in 2024. Ottawa said in a briefing note that more than 125,000 of a targeted 250,000 new spaces had been created by October 2025, against a March 2026 deadline.

Care & health · Germany

Germany’s care bill is paid through the payslip, and it is rising

Nursery fees are low and university is nearly free, the video notes. But health, care, pension and jobless insurance together take about 42% of gross pay, split between worker and employer.

ReportedReports, 2025–2026
Why it matters
Germany shows the cost of care doesn’t vanish when it is collectivized. It moves from fees to payroll, where it weighs on take-home pay and hiring.
Read more· full story & 3 sources

Germany funds much of what the video calls “care” through social insurance contributions, split roughly equally between employers and workers. In July 2025, tagesschau reported that combined contributions were already about 42% of gross pay. Government economic adviser Martin Werding said the total would reach 50% at some point without reform as the population ages, and could hit 45% by the end of the current parliament. A payroll reference site lists the combined 2027 rate at 42.3%, including an average health top-up of 2.9%. The video argues that this is where Germany’s squeeze hides: families pay little at the nursery door but see it in net pay, while the economy stalled in 2023–25.

Note: Werding’s figures are forecasts, not current rates. The 2027 rate comes from a payroll reference site and partly reflects planned changes.

Money & mood · All four · Germany

The “vibecession” may be partly a measurement gap: inflation figures leave out the cost of borrowing

Research co-written by Larry Summers finds that adding interest costs to inflation explains about three-quarters of 2023’s gap between US sentiment and the official numbers. An IMF study adds a second partial explanation for the 2022 surge.

AnalysisResearch, 2023–2024
Why it matters
When people say things feel worse than the data show, they may be partly right about the data. Rate rises hit borrowers in ways the headline inflation rate doesn’t capture.
Read more· full story & 4 sources

In an NBER working paper, Marijn Bolhuis, Judd Cramer, Karl Oskar Schulz and Lawrence Summers argue that the official consumer price index has left out mortgage interest since a 1983 redesign. It therefore misses the pain of higher rates on mortgages, car loans and credit cards. Adding those costs accounts for almost three-quarters of the 2023 gap between US consumer sentiment and what unemployment and inflation would predict. Across ten rich economies, sentiment gaps tracked rises in interest rates and were large in Germany, Spain and Austria. On the separate “greedy companies” theory, a 2023 IMF study found that domestic profits accounted for about 45% of euro-area inflation from early 2022 to early 2023. Import prices accounted for about 40% and labor costs for about 25%. Both explain a recent surge. As the video notes, neither explains why housing and care costs have been climbing for decades.
