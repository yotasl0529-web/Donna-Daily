COP31 climate talks collapse without a deal after the final text left out any mention of fossil fuels

Official statementBBC News, CBC News and 2 more · Nov 22–23 (overtime talks covered Nov 21)

The longest UN climate summit on record ended in Antalya, Turkey, on Sunday, Nov. 22, with no main political agreement, the first such collapse since Copenhagen in 2009.

Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisCOPs work by consensus, so a handful of oil producers can block language the majority wants, and this time the majority refused to accept a weaker deal rather than paper over the split. The failure does not cancel the Paris Agreement or countries’ own plans, but it leaves no new global signal on fossil fuels or money for climate-hit countries just as the world is expected to pass 1.5C of warming. Expect pressure to reform how COPs make decisions.
Full story, sources & checks4 sources

The longest UN climate summit on record ended in Antalya, Turkey, on Sunday, Nov. 22, with no main political agreement, the first such collapse since Copenhagen in 2009. Talks were due to finish on Friday but ran into Sunday. A last-minute draft presented early Sunday did not contain the words “fossil fuels” at all; an earlier draft had mentioned them six times, which oil producers rejected, the BBC reported. The European Union, speaking through Irish minister Darragh O’Brien and EU climate commissioner Wopke Hoekstra, refused to endorse a text that “does not even mention fossil fuels”, and Panama, the Marshall Islands, Vanuatu and others also objected. Poorer countries also complained that the text set no concrete figures for adaptation finance. Saudi Arabia’s delegate said Arab states had negotiated in good faith; Turkey’s COP31 president Murat Kurum said “there will be no new text”. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was “deeply disappointed”. Some side agreements survived: progress on a “just transition” mechanism, and more than 80 countries backed a target for electricity to meet 35% of global energy use by 2035. On Monday, Nov. 23, China’s foreign ministry called the outcome disappointing but said the green transition is “irreversible”.

Note: Updates the Nov 21 edition, which reported the talks running past their Friday deadline. The 35%-electrification goal (which China backed, Nov 21 edition) is a voluntary target, not part of a binding deal. China’s reaction comes from RTHK’s agency-based report of a foreign ministry briefing.

Official figuresOfficial statementHong Kong Free Press, RTHK (government-funded broadcaster) · Nov 22–23

Only 5,709 people were eligible to vote on Sunday, Nov. 22, for the body that will choose Hong Kong’s next chief executive; 5,640 cast ballots, officials said.

More2 sources
Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisThis tiny electorate, not the public, decides who leads Hong Kong. Record turnout among a few thousand pre-vetted voters says little about public opinion, but the upsets hint at competition within the pro-Beijing camp ahead of next year’s leadership contest.

Only 5,709 people were eligible to vote on Sunday, Nov. 22, for the body that will choose Hong Kong’s next chief executive; 5,640 cast ballots, officials said, for 723 contested seats across 31 subsectors, with 856 candidates. Hong Kong has about 4.1 million registered voters overall. Together with uncontested, ex-officio and designated seats, the winners form the 1,500-member Election Committee, which will pick the chief executive next year and elect 40 of the 90 lawmakers in 2029. Winners included former justice secretary Rimsky Yuen, Richard Li and Daryl Ng, but Law Society president Roden Tong and General Chamber of Commerce chief executive Patrick Yeung lost, Hong Kong Free Press reported. Chief Executive John Lee called it “healthy competition among patriots”, and on Monday, Nov. 23, Beijing’s Hong Kong and Macao Affairs Office said it showed the “fair competition of the new electoral system”. It was the second such race since Beijing’s 2021 overhaul to ensure only “patriots” govern; critics note much of the former opposition is jailed, in exile or has quit politics.
