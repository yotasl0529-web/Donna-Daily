Canada’s top court on Friday, Nov. 27, upheld nearly all of Quebec’s Bill 21, the 2019 secularism law that bars teachers, police officers and other public employees in positions of authority from wearing religious symbols at work. The one exception: the ban cannot apply to members of Quebec’s National Assembly, because that would infringe elected officials’ democratic rights. “Bill 21 is rooted in Quebec’s legitimate and historical commitment to ensuring the laicity of the state in a free and democratic society,” Chief Justice Richard Wagner wrote, according to CBC News. Quebec shielded the law with the Charter’s notwithstanding clause, which lets legislatures override some rights for renewable five-year periods; the court upheld that use. CBC reports the seven judges dismissed all the appeals and split 4–3 on whether the law violates the Charter’s guarantee of equality between the sexes (Section 28), with three dissenters saying it does; the BBC reports the court also said courts may still declare whether a law is consistent with the Charter even when the clause is invoked, but declined to do so here. Quebec Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon called the ruling “a victory for secularism,” AP reported. Prime Minister Mark Carney said he respected the decision and, per AP, pledged his government would not use the override; Manitoba Premier Wab Kinew called Bill 21 “legislative racism.” The National Council of Canadian Muslims called the law discriminatory.

Note: Follows the Nov 26 and Nov 27 editions, which reported that the ruling was scheduled. Outlets describe the judges’ splits differently because the court answered about ten separate questions with different majorities; this summary uses CBC’s account of the 4–3 split on Section 28. The ruling itself was not read in full for this edition.

Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisFlag offences are politically charged in Hong Kong, so a unanimous ruling that prosecutors must prove deliberate intent sets a clear limit on how the law can be applied — especially to people whose disabilities explain their actions. It is a reminder that the top court can still check lower courts on the facts.

Hong Kong’s Court of Final Appeal on Friday, Nov. 27, unanimously quashed the convictions of a young man with autism for desecrating the national and Hong Kong flags, ruling the prosecution never proved he intended to insult the flags as symbols. Wong Chun-lok, then 19, pulled down one national flag and four regional flags in Sham Shui Po on National Day in 2022 by bending their flagpoles, and was sentenced to a rehabilitation centre. He was diagnosed with autism at three and also has ADHD and hypersensitivity to visual stimuli; his mother testified about a similar 2018 incident in which he pulled down shop banners that disturbed him. Delivering the judgment, Justice Joseph Fok held that both offences require proof of intent to undermine the flag’s dignity as a symbol — recklessness is not enough — and called the earlier appeal judge’s analysis “wholly inadequate,” RTHK reported.
