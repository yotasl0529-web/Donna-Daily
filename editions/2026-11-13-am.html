Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisOutlook and Teams sit under the email, calendars and classes of millions of schools, offices and governments, so a two-day outage shows how much daily life now depends on a single company’s cloud — and how slowly that kind of failure can be fixed.

A Microsoft outage that began shortly before 1 p.m. ET on Thursday was still disrupting Outlook and other Microsoft 365 services early Friday. Microsoft said it saw no sign of malicious activity and reported gradual, uneven recovery. CNBC said Outlook, Teams and Microsoft 365 services such as SharePoint and Copilot were hit, along with outside services that connect to Outlook Calendar. In an update at about 5 a.m. ET Friday, Microsoft said users might still be unable to connect to several Microsoft 365 products, including Outlook on the web and desktop, and gave no timeline for full service; most access to Teams had returned. The company initially linked the problem to elevated load after a number of servers became unexpectedly unavailable, and said the failed automated health checks may originate from a specific component that processes server health. It said it was bringing affected systems back in stages and extending the retry window for delayed emails to up to 48 hours. The Guardian’s live coverage said Microsoft later reported “positive results for server stability” after a software patch but still listed service degradation, and that government agencies in Australia, Japan and the United States were among those affected. The Canadian Press said thousands in Canada were affected. Microsoft shares were 2.6% lower in premarket trading, CNBC said.

Note: Microsoft’s explanation of the cause is its own preliminary account. Recovery was still in progress when this edition was compiled; service may differ by region and account.

McGill researchers find a deepfake network using stolen influencers’ faces to spread trade-war falsehoods to Canadians

AnalysisCentre for Media, Technology and Democracy (McGill), The Canadian Press via CP24 · Nov 12

Researchers at McGill University’s Centre for Media, Technology and Democracy said Thursday they had found a network of at least 293 TikTok and Instagram accounts that take real creators’ videos and use AI to redraw their lips and add synthetic voices, making them appear to talk about Canadian politics.

More2 sources
Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisFakes built from real people’s faces are harder for platforms and viewers to spot, and this network targeted exactly the issues — trade, tariffs, Alberta — on which Canadians are making political decisions.

Between Aug. 7 and Nov. 4 the accounts posted 3,201 videos with 22.1 million plays, much of it about the U.S.–Canada trade war and Alberta’s referendum. One false story — a non-existent 48-hour ultimatum from President Donald Trump, to which Prime Minister Mark Carney supposedly responded by cancelling $8 billion in U.S. defence contracts — was shared in 161 videos and played 5.1 million times, the report said. TikTok labelled only 4% of the videos as AI, and only 6.3% of commenters flagged them as fake. The researchers said the network is “almost certainly” run from outside North America; some accounts carry Chinese-language settings or a registration in China, which they called “the only lead we have on a country.” They said it does not push any government’s line and has not been monetized, and its purpose is unclear.

Note: The origin and purpose assessments are the researchers’ own analysis; they say who runs the network is not known.

A bronze leg found in a Scottish bog anchors Edinburgh’s biggest-ever exhibition on Roman Scotland

AnalysisThe Guardian, National Museums Scotland · Nov 12 · opens Sat, Nov 14

A gilded bronze leg dug out of a bog in the Scottish Borders in 1820 — long thought to have been looted from England and carried north by raiding tribes — is now believed to have come from a huge statue of Emperor Antoninus Pius on horseback erected near Hawick around AD 140, the Guardian reported.

File photo, 2026 · Photo: Iain Lees · CC BY-SA 2.0

More2 sources
Why it matters · Donna Daily analysisIt recasts a familiar story — that Rome stopped at Hadrian’s Wall — and shows how new research on old finds can change national history.

It is the centrepiece of “Roman Scotland: Life on the Edge of Empire,” which the Guardian describes as the largest exhibition ever staged on the Romans in Scotland; it opens Saturday at the National Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh. Principal curator Fraser Hunter argues the Romans were a far bigger force north of Hadrian’s Wall than once thought. The show also includes a reconstructed decorated bowl and two altars from the Mithras temple at the Roman fort at Inveresk, east of Edinburgh, according to the museum.
